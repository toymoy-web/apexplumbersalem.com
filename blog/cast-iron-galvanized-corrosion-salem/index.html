Cast Iron and Galvanized Pipe Corrosion in Salem's Historic Homes

Interior corrosion in cast iron drain lines and galvanized supply lines develops slowly, showing up first as water quality and pressure problems long before an actual leak.

By the Salem Apex Plumbing team · Updated 2026-09-08

Rusty water from the tap, low pressure that's gotten worse over the years, or a persistent metallic smell are all signs pointing to the same underlying cause in many of Salem's older homes. Interior supply and drain pipes made of cast iron or galvanized steel have been corroding from the inside for decades. Unlike a buried sewer lateral, these are pipes running through your walls and floors, and understanding what's actually happening inside them helps explain why the problem tends to get worse rather than better over time.

This is a genuinely different situation from what happens underground. A failing sewer lateral affects waste leaving the home, while corroding supply and drain lines affect the water you're actually using every day, which is part of why the symptoms show up differently and why the fix requires a different approach.

The Two Materials

Cast Iron and Galvanized Steel Fail Differently

Cast iron was commonly used for drain, waste, and vent lines in homes built through the mid-20th century, valued for its durability and sound-dampening properties. Over decades, though, cast iron corrodes and can develop rough, scaled interior surfaces that catch debris and eventually crack. Galvanized steel, used for supply lines, has a zinc coating meant to prevent rust, but that coating degrades over time, and once it's gone, the steel underneath corrodes from the inside out, gradually narrowing the pipe's interior diameter.

Why It Shows Up as Water Quality Problems First

Corrosion Affects What Comes Out of the Tap

Rusty or Discolored Water

Corrosion particles breaking loose into the water supply, especially after periods of low use.

Metallic Taste or Smell

A sign the water is picking up corrosion byproducts as it travels through the pipe.

Declining Water Pressure

Interior corrosion narrows the pipe's effective diameter over time, restricting flow.

Pinhole Leaks in Multiple Spots

Advanced corrosion weakening the pipe wall at several points rather than just one.

These symptoms often develop so gradually that homeowners adjust without realizing anything has changed, running the tap longer before it clears or just accepting lower pressure as normal for the house. By the time the problem is severe enough to prompt a service call, the underlying corrosion has usually been progressing for years.

Repair Versus Whole-Home Solutions

Why Patching One Leak Rarely Solves the Real Problem

A single pinhole leak in a galvanized line can be patched, but if the pipe is corroding at that spot, it's corroding at a similar rate throughout the rest of the system. Homeowners who patch leak after leak over a period of years often spend close to the cost of full repiping anyway, just spread across multiple stressful emergency calls rather than one planned project. Our repiping and whole-home pipe replacement team sees this pattern regularly and can walk through the real cost comparison for a specific home's situation.

What About Drain Lines Specifically

Cast Iron Drain Problems Show Up Differently

While galvanized supply line corrosion shows up as water quality and pressure issues, cast iron drain line corrosion tends to show up as recurring clogs and slow drainage, since the rough, scaled interior surface catches debris far more readily than smooth modern PVC. A camera inspection through our drain cleaning and unclogging service can confirm whether a persistent drain problem is a corrosion issue rather than a simple, resolvable clog.

A Reasonable Approach for Older Homes

You Don't Need to Repipe Preventively, But You Should Know Where You Stand

Not every home with cast iron or galvanized pipe needs immediate replacement. Plenty of older systems are still functioning adequately, just with the understanding that they're operating on borrowed time relative to modern materials. The reasonable approach for a Salem home with original plumbing from this era is to pay attention to the early warning signs, water discoloration, declining pressure, an uptick in leak frequency, rather than waiting for a dramatic failure to force the decision. Catching the pattern early gives you the option to plan a repiping project on your own timeline instead of responding to it as an emergency.

If you're planning a kitchen or bathroom remodel in a home with original galvanized or cast iron plumbing, that project is often a practical opportunity to address the pipe question at the same time. Walls are already open, and some of the labor cost of accessing the pipe is already part of the remodel work regardless.

FAQs

Common Questions

Is rusty water from my tap dangerous?

It's generally more of an aesthetic and taste issue than a health hazard, but persistent discoloration is worth having evaluated since it signals ongoing pipe corrosion that will continue to worsen.

How do I know if my home has galvanized or cast iron pipe?

Home age is a strong indicator for pre-1980s construction, but a plumber can confirm pipe material during any service visit, often visible at an accessible section in a basement or crawl space.

Is it worth repiping preventively if I haven't had major problems yet?

Not necessarily immediately, but tracking early warning signs like declining pressure or occasional discoloration helps you plan a repiping project on your own schedule rather than reacting to an emergency.
