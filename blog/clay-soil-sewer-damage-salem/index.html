Why Salem's Volcanic Clay Soil Cracks Sewer Lines

The Willamette Valley's clay-heavy soil swells and shrinks with the seasons, and buried sewer pipe takes the brunt of it. Here's what's actually happening underground.

By the Salem Apex Plumbing team · Updated 2026-08-18

If you've lived in Salem for more than a few years, you've probably noticed that sewer and drain problems seem to cluster in certain seasons, or that the same section of pipe keeps causing trouble no matter how many times it's been cleared. There's a real, physical reason for that, and it comes down to what's actually underneath your yard: volcanic clay soil that behaves very differently across Salem's wet winters and dry summers.

The Geology

Why Salem's Soil Moves the Way It Does

The Willamette Valley floor beneath Marion County formed from ancient basalt lava flows, which weathered over time into iron-rich clay soil. This soil type, part of the broader Jory soil series common throughout the valley's hill country, has a defining characteristic: it swells significantly when it absorbs water and shrinks again as it dries out. Salem receives roughly 40 inches of rain a year, with the heaviest concentration between October and March, which means the soil beneath most yards goes through a real expansion and contraction cycle every single year.

That cycle doesn't just happen at the surface. It extends down to the depth where sewer and water lines are buried, and buried pipe doesn't get to just sit still while the soil around it moves.

What This Does to Buried Pipe

From Soil Movement to a Cracked Sewer Line

Joint Separation

Repeated soil movement can pull pipe sections apart at their connection points.

Bellied Sections

Uneven settling creates a low spot in the line where waste and water pool.

Cracked Fittings

Rigid materials like clay tile and cast iron can crack under sustained pressure.

New Entry Points for Roots

Every new gap or crack becomes an opportunity for root intrusion.

None of these happen overnight. A sewer line that was installed perfectly straight and level can develop a belly or a separated joint gradually, over years of repeated seasonal cycling, long before anyone notices a symptom inside the house. By the time a homeowner sees a slow drain or a backup, the underlying soil-driven damage has often been progressing for a while.

Why This Compounds With Root Intrusion

Clay Soil and Mature Trees Are a Bad Combination

Salem's older neighborhoods, including Highland, Grant, South Salem, and Morningside, are lined with mature Oregon white oaks and Douglas firs, and those trees make the clay soil problem noticeably worse. Roots actively search out moisture, and a joint that's separated even slightly from soil movement is exactly the kind of opening a root system will exploit. Once roots get inside a pipe, they branch out and trap sediment and debris, turning a structural problem into a recurring clog problem at the same time.

What to Watch For

Early Signs of Soil-Driven Sewer Damage

Multiple slow drains throughout the house, rather than just one, is often the first sign that something structural is happening in the main line rather than a routine localized clog. A soggy or unusually green patch of yard above where the line runs can indicate a leak from a cracked or separated section. Recurring clogs that come back within weeks of being cleared, despite no obvious cause, are a strong signal that the underlying issue is structural rather than just buildup.

Diagnosis and Fix

A Camera Tells You What's Actually Happening

A video camera inspection is the only way to see the real condition of a sewer line rather than guessing based on symptoms. It shows whether the damage is a single separated joint, correctable with a spot repair, or a bellied section that's holding standing water and needs more extensive work. Our sewer line repair team uses camera inspection on every call before recommending anything, since the fix genuinely depends on what the actual damage looks like.

For lines where root intrusion has combined with soil movement to create a real mess, hydro jetting is often part of the fix, clearing root mass and sediment that a standard cable snake would leave behind. And for a line that's deteriorated too far along its length to repair section by section, our sewer line replacement team handles a full lateral swap with modern pipe that's less vulnerable to the same soil movement.

What Homeowners Can Actually Do

You Can't Stop the Soil From Moving, But You Can Get Ahead of It

There's no way to change how Salem's clay soil behaves, but a proactive camera inspection every few years, particularly for a home with mature trees near the sewer line, can catch a developing problem before it becomes an emergency backup. This is especially worth considering for a home that's had even one prior sewer issue, since a line that's already shown one weak point is more likely to develop another given the same soil conditions working on it year after year.

Beyond Sewer Lines

Water Lines Face the Same Pressure

Sewer laterals get most of the attention because a backup is immediately obvious, but the same clay soil movement affects buried water supply lines just as much. A water line stressed by years of soil expansion and contraction can develop a pinhole leak or a cracked fitting the same way a sewer line does. It often shows up first as unexplained low pressure or a wet patch in the yard, rather than a dramatic failure. Our water line repair and replacement team sees this pattern regularly, and it's diagnosed the same way: locate the actual damage first, then decide between a targeted repair and a full line replacement based on what's really there.

The practical takeaway for any Salem homeowner is that clay soil doesn't discriminate between pipe types. If your sewer line has shown structural wear from soil movement, it's worth asking whether your water service line, running through the same soil conditions, might be due for a look as well.

FAQs

Common Questions

Can I prevent clay soil from damaging my sewer line?

You can't change the soil's behavior, but regular camera inspections can catch developing damage before it becomes an emergency, especially in homes with mature trees near the line.

Is this specific to Salem, or does it happen elsewhere?

The same Jory soil formation extends throughout much of the Willamette Valley's hill country, so nearby communities on similar soil see related issues.

How often should I have my sewer line inspected if I've never had a problem?

Every few years is reasonable for a home with mature trees near the line or original pipe from before the 1980s. A home with a completely clean history and modern pipe needs it less often.
