Hard Water From the North Santiam River: What It Does to Your Water Heater

Salem's municipal water is safe and well-treated, but it still carries mineral content that builds up inside tank and tankless water heaters over years of use.

By the Salem Apex Plumbing team · Updated 2026-08-25

If your water heater seems to need repairs more often than you'd expect, or if you've noticed white, chalky buildup around faucets and showerheads, the culprit is very likely dissolved minerals in Salem's water supply working on your plumbing system year after year. Understanding what's actually happening inside your water heater can help you decide whether it's worth addressing the root cause or just keeping up with repairs as they come.

The Source

Where Salem's Water Actually Comes From

Salem's municipal water is drawn from the North Santiam River and treated through one of the largest slow-sand filtration systems in the country. The treatment process does a good job removing sediment, pathogens, and contaminants, but it doesn't remove dissolved minerals like calcium and magnesium, the compounds responsible for what's commonly called hard water. That mineral content travels through the entire municipal system and into every home connected to it.

This isn't a treatment failure or a quality issue with Salem's water system. Removing hardness minerals isn't part of what municipal drinking water treatment is designed to do anywhere, since hardness is a water quality preference issue rather than a health or safety concern. The slow-sand filtration system does exactly what it's meant to do, providing safe, clean drinking water. Addressing hardness is a separate, optional step that happens at the home level, not the treatment plant level.

What Happens Inside the Tank

Sediment Buildup and Reduced Efficiency

Inside a standard tank water heater, dissolved minerals settle to the bottom as sediment over time. That sediment layer acts as insulation between the heating element and the water above it, forcing the unit to work harder and longer to reach the same temperature. Left unaddressed for years, this buildup can significantly shorten a tank's usable life and increase energy costs in the process, since the unit is essentially fighting against its own accumulated mineral deposits every time it heats. In more severe cases, the sediment layer can also insulate the tank bottom enough to cause overheating and premature failure of the heating element itself, turning a maintenance issue into an actual repair call.

Tankless water heaters experience a related but different problem. Instead of settling as loose sediment, minerals scale directly onto the heat exchanger surface, gradually restricting water flow and reducing heating efficiency. This is why tankless units specifically need periodic descaling, a maintenance step tank units don't require in the same way.

Signs to Watch For

How Mineral Buildup Actually Shows Up

Popping or Rumbling Noises

Sediment cracking and shifting as the tank heats is a classic early sign.

Longer Time to Heat

Insulating sediment forces the unit to run longer for the same result.

Reduced Hot Water Volume

Sediment takes up tank space that would otherwise hold usable hot water.

Fixture Scale Buildup

Visible white deposits on faucets and showerheads point to the same mineral source.

What Actually Helps

Maintenance vs. Treatment at the Source

An annual tank flush is the single most effective maintenance step for a standard water heater, clearing accumulated sediment before it has a chance to meaningfully insulate the heating element. This is a straightforward service that pays for itself in extended tank life and better efficiency, and it's worth doing on a schedule rather than waiting until symptoms appear. Our water heater repair team includes tank flushing as part of routine maintenance visits, separate from an actual repair call.

For households seeing persistent mineral issues across multiple fixtures, not just the water heater, a whole-house water softener addresses the root cause rather than the downstream symptoms. Our water softener installation team tests actual water hardness before recommending a system, since the right-sized softener depends on real numbers rather than an assumption based on general Salem water quality.

Repair or Replace

When Mineral Damage Means It's Time for a New Unit

A water heater under about 8 years old with sediment-related symptoms is usually a good candidate for a flush and continued service. A unit that's already past 10 to 12 years, especially one that's never been flushed, is a different situation. At that point, the internal tank corrosion that often accompanies years of sediment buildup means replacement is typically the more reliable long-term choice, even though a flush might provide temporary improvement. Our water heater installation and replacement team can walk through the honest comparison for your specific unit's age and condition.

The Bottom Line

Prevention Is Cheaper Than Reaction

Salem's water quality is safe to drink and generally well-regarded, but the same mineral content that makes it safe also makes routine water heater maintenance more important here than it would be in a location with genuinely soft water. A yearly flush costs far less than an early tank replacement, and for households seeing the problem show up across multiple fixtures, a water softener addresses the mineral content directly rather than treating each symptom separately as it appears.

A Related Consideration

Mineral Buildup Affects More Than Just the Water Heater

The same dissolved minerals that scale a water heater's heat exchanger also wear down faucet cartridges, O-rings, and valve seats throughout a home over years of normal use. A faucet that's developed a persistent drip or lost pressure is often showing the same underlying cause as a water heater needing more frequent service, just at a different fixture. Our faucet and sink repair team sees this pattern regularly, and it's part of why we ask about water heater history when diagnosing a fixture issue and vice versa. Treating the mineral content at the source with a softener addresses both problems at once rather than chasing symptoms fixture by fixture.

FAQs

Common Questions

How often should I flush my water heater?

Annually is the standard recommendation for homes on Salem's municipal water supply, given the mineral content in the source water.

Will a water softener eliminate the need for tank flushing?

It significantly reduces the mineral load reaching your water heater, but periodic flushing is still good practice since some sediment can still accumulate over time.

Is Salem's water actually unsafe because of the minerals?

No, mineral content affecting fixtures and appliances is a hardness issue, not a safety issue. Salem's water meets safety standards; hardness is about scale and buildup, not health risk.
