Orangeburg Sewer Pipe: What Older Salem Homes Still Have Underground

A wood-pulp sewer material used from the 1940s through the early 1970s, and why Salem's clay soil makes it a genuinely higher-risk situation than in other climates.

By the Salem Apex Plumbing team · Updated 2026-08-15

If your Salem home was built between the 1940s and the early 1970s and you've never had the sewer lateral inspected, there's a real chance it's still running on Orangeburg pipe. It's not a defect that shows up on a home inspection checklist the way a cracked foundation does, and most homeowners never think about their sewer line until it stops working. But Orangeburg pipe has a predictable failure pattern, and knowing what it is and what to watch for can save you from a surprise backup at the worst possible time.

What It Actually Is

Orangeburg Pipe, Explained

Orangeburg pipe is a sewer material made from wood pulp layers impregnated with coal tar pitch, manufactured under that name from roughly the 1940s through the early 1970s. It was cheap and easy to install compared to cast iron or clay, which made it popular during the postwar building boom across the country, including in Salem's growing neighborhoods during that era. The problem is that wood pulp, even treated with pitch, isn't a permanent material. Given enough time and enough moisture, it softens, deforms, and eventually collapses.

Why Salem Homes Are Especially at Risk

Clay Soil Makes a Bad Material Worse

Salem sits on volcanic clay soil that expands when it absorbs winter rain and contracts through the dry summer months. That seasonal movement puts constant pressure on buried pipe, and it's a rough environment for a material that's already prone to softening in wet conditions. Orangeburg pipe under a Salem yard isn't just dealing with age, it's dealing with decades of soil that's been pushing and pulling on it every winter and summer since it went into the ground.

Older neighborhoods built out during the Orangeburg era, including parts of Highland, Grant, and sections of West Salem, are where we most often find this pipe still in service. If your home falls into that era and that general area, it's worth having a camera inspection done even if you haven't had any problems yet.

A Note on Other Materials From the Same Era

Orangeburg Isn't the Only Old Material Underground

Homes from the same construction window sometimes have clay tile sewer laterals instead of Orangeburg, which fail differently but for related reasons. Clay tile is more rigid than Orangeburg and doesn't soften the same way, but it's brittle, and Salem's clay soil movement can crack it at the joints between sections. Root intrusion is also more common in clay tile than Orangeburg, since roots exploit the small gaps at each joint rather than pushing through a softened pipe wall. Either material points to the same underlying issue: pipe from before modern PVC became standard, reaching the end of a reasonable service life in soil conditions that accelerate wear.

How It Fails

Signs Your Sewer Line May Be Orangeburg Pipe in Trouble

Recurring Backups

A line that clogs repeatedly despite regular cleaning often has a structural cause, not just buildup.

Slow Drains Throughout the House

A softened, oval-shaped section of Orangeburg pipe restricts flow even without a full blockage.

Sunken or Soggy Yard Patch

A collapsed section beneath the surface can cause visible settling above it.

Sewage Odor Outdoors

A failing pipe wall can let sewage escape into the surrounding soil.

Unlike a cracked cast iron pipe, which tends to fail at a specific joint or crack, Orangeburg pipe often deteriorates more gradually across its whole length. That means the failure pattern can look different: not one dramatic collapse, but a slow flattening of the pipe's round cross-section into an oval one, which quietly restricts flow for months before anyone notices a real problem.

What to Do About It

Diagnosis Comes Before Any Fix

A video camera inspection is the only reliable way to confirm whether a line is Orangeburg pipe and how deteriorated it actually is. That inspection tells a technician whether the pipe is holding its shape reasonably well, in which case a trenchless lining might extend its life significantly, or whether it's already deformed and collapsing, in which case replacement is the more honest recommendation. Guessing at the condition from the home's age alone isn't a substitute for actually looking.

If the camera shows a line still in reasonably round shape with only isolated soft spots, our sewer line repair team can often address it with trenchless lining or a targeted spot repair, avoiding a full excavation. If the deterioration is widespread along the pipe's length, which is common on Orangeburg lines that have been in the ground since the 1950s or 60s, replacement is usually the more reliable fix. Our sewer line replacement team handles a full lateral swap in that case, typically with a modern PVC line that won't share the same failure mode.

If You're Buying or Selling

Why This Matters for Real Estate Transactions

A standard home inspection doesn't typically include a sewer camera inspection unless specifically requested, which means a home with failing Orangeburg pipe can pass a general inspection with no red flags. If you're buying an older Salem home, particularly one from the 1940s through early 1970s construction window, requesting a sewer camera inspection is a reasonable condition of the sale. It's a relatively small cost against the possibility of a full sewer line replacement shortly after moving in.

If you're selling a home from that era, having a camera inspection done ahead of listing can either give you peace of mind to represent the line honestly, or give you the chance to address it on your own timeline. That's almost always better than dealing with it during a tight closing window.

The Bottom Line

Age and Location Are the Real Indicators

Not every Salem home from this era has Orangeburg pipe, and not every Orangeburg line is in immediate danger of collapsing. But the combination of the material's known weaknesses and Salem's clay soil movement makes this a genuinely higher-risk situation than the same pipe would be in a different climate. A camera inspection is inexpensive relative to the cost of an emergency sewer backup, and it turns a guessing game into an actual answer about what's under your yard.

FAQs

Common Questions

How do I know if my home has Orangeburg pipe?

The clearest way is a video camera inspection of the sewer line. Homes built between the 1940s and early 1970s in areas with that era's construction are the most likely candidates.

Can Orangeburg pipe be repaired, or does it always need replacement?

It depends on condition. A line that's still reasonably round with isolated soft spots can sometimes be repaired with trenchless lining. Widespread deformation usually means replacement is the more reliable fix.

Is a sewer camera inspection included in a standard home inspection?

Usually not. It's typically a separate request, which is worth making for an older home during a real estate transaction.
