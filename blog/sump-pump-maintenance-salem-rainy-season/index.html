Sump Pump Maintenance Before Salem's Rainy Season Hits

A few minutes of maintenance before October can be the difference between a sump pump that works when it matters and one that fails during the storm it was installed to handle.

By the Salem Apex Plumbing team · Updated 2026-08-28

Marion County gets roughly 40 inches of rain a year, with the heaviest concentration falling between October and March. For homes with a crawl space or basement, that rainy season is exactly when a neglected sump pump gets tested, often at the worst possible moment. A few maintenance steps before the rain really sets in can be the difference between a pump that quietly does its job and one that fails during the storm it was installed to handle.

Why This Matters Now

Sump Pumps Fail Quietly Until They Don't

A sump pump that's working fine can look identical to one that's about to fail, right up until the moment it's actually needed during a heavy rain event. A stuck float switch, a clogged check valve, or a motor nearing the end of its service life often shows no obvious symptoms during normal, light use. The only real way to know a pump will perform when it matters is to test it deliberately, not just assume it's fine because nothing has gone wrong recently.

This matters more in Salem-area homes than it might elsewhere, given how concentrated the region's rainfall actually is. Roughly two-thirds of that 40 inches of annual rain falls in the five months between October and March. That means a sump pump here isn't dealing with rain spread evenly across the year, it's dealing with sustained, repeated demand across a fairly compressed stretch of months.

The Pre-Season Checklist

What to Check Before the Rain Sets In

Test the Float Switch

Pour water into the basin and confirm the pump activates and shuts off properly.

Clear the Basin

Remove any debris that's accumulated since the last check, which can interfere with the float or intake.

Check the Discharge Line

Confirm water is actually being routed away from the foundation, not just recirculating nearby.

Verify the Check Valve

A failing check valve lets water flow back into the basin after each pump cycle, forcing it to run more than necessary.

Each of these checks takes only a few minutes, but skipping them means the first real test of the season is an actual storm, which is not when you want to discover a problem.

Backup Power

What Happens When the Power Goes Out

A standard sump pump depends entirely on grid power, which means it stops working during exactly the kind of severe weather event most likely to also cause an outage. A battery backup system keeps the pump running through a power interruption. It's worth genuine consideration for any property that's seen prior flooding or groundwater issues, since the storms most likely to overwhelm a sump pump's capacity are often the same storms most likely to knock out power in the area.

Signs You Need More Than Maintenance

When a Working Pump Isn't Enough

Sometimes the issue isn't a maintenance gap, it's that the installed pump was never sized correctly for the property's actual groundwater conditions. A pump that runs constantly even after basic maintenance, or one that can't keep up despite functioning correctly, may simply be undersized. Our sump pump installation and repair team evaluates actual groundwater volume before recommending a system, rather than defaulting to a generic capacity that may not match your specific property.

Property location plays a real role here too. A home closer to the Willamette River or one of its tributary creeks typically deals with higher groundwater volume than a home on higher ground farther from any waterway, even within the same general Salem-area climate. That's part of why a generic, one-size-fits-all pump recommendation is less useful than an actual assessment of your specific property's conditions.

If a pump has already failed and caused water intrusion, that's a different, more urgent situation than routine maintenance. Our water damage restoration team handles extraction and drying after the fact, but catching a failing pump before that happens is almost always the better outcome, both for the home and for the cost involved.

A Simple Seasonal Habit

Make It an Annual Routine

The most effective sump pump maintenance isn't complicated, it's consistent. Testing the system once before the rainy season begins, and again partway through if the winter turns out to be a wet one, catches most problems before they become emergencies. For homes with a documented history of groundwater issues, a professional inspection on the same seasonal schedule adds a level of assurance that a homeowner's own basin test can't fully replace, particularly for checking motor condition and overall pump capacity against current groundwater patterns.

FAQs

Common Questions

How often should I test my sump pump?

Once before the rainy season begins in the fall, and again partway through the winter if it's been an especially wet one, catches most developing problems.

Do I need a battery backup if I already have a sump pump?

It's worth considering, especially if your property has a history of flooding or groundwater issues, since major storms often knock out power at the same time they stress the pump.

My sump pump runs constantly. Is that normal?

Not necessarily. It can mean the pump is undersized for your actual groundwater conditions, or that a check valve is letting water cycle back into the basin. Worth having evaluated rather than assumed normal.
