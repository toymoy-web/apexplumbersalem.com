Local Cause

When Whole-Home Repiping Makes Sense

Homes built before the 1980s in Salem's older neighborhoods, including Highland, Grant, and parts of South Salem, often still have original galvanized steel supply lines. Galvanized pipe corrodes from the inside out over decades, narrowing the interior diameter and eventually developing pinhole leaks at multiple points rather than one isolated spot. Once a home starts seeing recurring leaks in different locations, patching each one individually usually costs more over time than repiping the whole system at once.

Signs

Signs You Need Whole-Home Repiping

Recurring Leaks in Different Spots

Pinhole leaks appearing in multiple locations rather than one isolated failure.

Discolored Water Throughout the House

Rust-colored water from multiple fixtures points to widespread pipe corrosion.

Low Water Pressure Sitewide

Corrosion narrowing the interior of galvanized pipe restricts flow throughout the home.

Original Galvanized or Polybutylene Pipe

Materials from before the 1980s that are past their realistic service life.

Metallic Taste or Smell

Corroding galvanized pipe can affect water taste and odor even before a visible leak appears.

The Process

How Whole-Home Repiping Works

1

Assess

Map every supply line and identify the full scope of replacement needed.

2

Plan Access

Identify the least invasive path through walls, floors, and crawl spaces.

3

Repipe

Install modern PEX or copper piping throughout the home.

4

Test & Patch

Pressure test the new system, then patch any opened walls or ceilings.

We typically repipe in sections to keep at least partial water service available during the project, and we coordinate wall and ceiling patching so you're not left with drywall repair as a separate headache afterward. Most homeowners are surprised by how little disruption a well-planned repipe actually causes compared to what they expected going in.

Repiping vs. Patch Repairs

Why Patching One Leak at a Time Costs More

Fixing each pinhole leak as it appears feels cheaper in the moment, but galvanized pipe that's corroding in one spot is corroding throughout the system at a similar rate. Homeowners who patch leak after leak over a few years often end up spending close to the cost of full repiping anyway, just spread across multiple emergency calls instead of one planned project with a predictable price. Repiping also resolves the low water pressure that corrosion causes throughout the house, which patching individual leaks never fixes.

All repiping work follows the Oregon Plumbing Specialty Code, based on the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC), by technicians licensed through Oregon's Building Codes Division.

FAQs

Common Questions

How long does whole-home repiping take?

Most homes take three to five days depending on size and access. We coordinate the schedule to minimize how long water service is interrupted.

Will I be without water during the project?

We repipe in sections where possible to keep at least partial service running, though some short interruptions are unavoidable during the switchover.

PEX or copper, which is better?

Both are solid modern choices. PEX costs less and resists freeze-cracking better, while copper has a longer track record and some homeowners prefer it for that reason. We'll walk through the real trade-offs for your home.

Do you patch the walls afterward?

Yes, drywall and ceiling patching after pipe access is included as part of the project.
