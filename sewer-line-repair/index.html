Local Cause

Why Salem Sewer Lines Fail

Salem's volcanic clay soil expands when it absorbs winter rain and contracts again through the dry summer months. That seasonal movement shifts buried sewer lines, separates joints, and cracks older pipe materials over time. Homes built before Salem's mid-century growth may still be running original clay tile laterals, or in some cases Orangeburg pipe, a wood-pulp and coal-tar material used from the 1940s through the early 1970s that softens and deforms in wet soil.

Mature Oregon white oaks and Douglas firs common in Highland, Grant, South Salem, and Morningside make the problem worse. Their root systems push into any crack or loose joint they find, which is why root intrusion and clay-soil movement are the two causes behind most of the sewer line repairs we handle.

Signs

Signs Your Sewer Line Needs Repair

Multiple Slow Drains

Every fixture in the house draining slowly points to the main line, not one drain.

Sewage Odor in the Yard

A smell near where the lateral runs often means a crack is letting sewage escape underground.

Soggy or Sunken Ground

A wet or settling patch of lawn above the line can mean the pipe has collapsed.

Recurring Backups

A line that keeps backing up despite regular cleaning usually has a structural problem, not just buildup.

Diagnosis

Video Camera Inspection First

Before recommending any repair, we run a video camera down the line to see the actual damage: a hairline crack, a root mass, a bellied section holding standing water, or a fully collapsed segment. That inspection tells us whether a targeted spot repair will solve the problem or whether the damage is extensive enough that replacement is the more honest recommendation.

Repair Options

Trenchless Lining vs. Spot Repair vs. Open-Cut

A localized crack or a single damaged joint is often fixable with a spot repair or a section of trenchless pipe lining, both of which avoid digging up your entire yard. Trenchless lining inserts a resin-saturated liner into the existing pipe that cures into a new pipe-within-a-pipe, which works well for cracks and small root intrusion points along an otherwise sound line.

When damage is more extensive, such as a badly bellied section or old Orangeburg pipe that's deformed along most of its length, open-cut excavation to replace that section directly is sometimes the more reliable fix. We'll always explain which category your situation falls into and why, rather than defaulting to the most expensive option. All repair work follows the Oregon Plumbing Specialty Code, which is based on the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC).

If Sewage Has Backed Up

Safety Before We Arrive

If sewage has backed up into the home, keep children and pets away from the affected area, since raw sewage carries real health risks. Avoid running any water in the house until the blockage is addressed, since that can push the backup further into the structure. Open a window if possible for ventilation, and don't attempt to clear a main line blockage yourself.

FAQs

Common Questions

How do I know if I need a repair or a full replacement?

A camera inspection tells us the difference. A single crack or short damaged section is usually a repair. Extensive deterioration along most of the line's length usually means replacement is the better long-term value.

What is Orangeburg pipe and should I be concerned?

Orangeburg is a wood-pulp and coal-tar sewer pipe used from the 1940s through the early 1970s. It deteriorates in wet soil over time, so if your home has aging plumbing and the lateral hasn't been inspected, a camera inspection is worth doing.

Will you dig up my whole yard to fix the line?

Not necessarily. Trenchless lining and spot repairs handle many situations without full excavation. We'll only recommend open-cut work when the damage genuinely requires it.

Is sewer line repair covered by insurance?

A sudden failure causing interior water damage is sometimes covered. Gradual deterioration from age or root intrusion typically isn't. Check your specific policy.
